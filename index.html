About the competition

Overview and Aim

In the past decade, dynamic constrained multiobjective optimization has attracted increasing research interest. Such problems are widely encountered in real-world applications, including scheduling optimization and resource allocation, where multiple objectives and constraints may vary over time.

Compared with dynamic multiobjective optimization problems or constrained multiobjective optimization problems alone, dynamic constrained multiobjective optimization problems (DCMOPs) exhibit more complex characteristics and pose additional challenges. Different environmental changes require effective response strategies, while changing constraints and limited response time make it difficult to identify high-quality feasible solution sets.

This competition aims to promote research on dynamic constrained multiobjective optimization (DCMO). Based on a test suite with various characteristics, researchers can better understand the strengths and weaknesses of dynamic constrained multiobjective evolutionary algorithms (DCMOEAs).

The benchmark suite

Test Problems

To promote the research on dynamic constrained multiobjective optimization (DCMO), 10 benchmark functions are employed from DCF [1], covering diverse characteristics which exactly represent different real-world scenarios, for example, continuity-disconnection, time-dependent PF/PS geometries, dynamic infeasible region, small feasible region, and so on.

The detailed definitions of these 10 test problems can be found in Benchmark Problems for CEC2023 Competition on Dynamic Constrained Multiobjective Optimization [1]. The technical report can be downloaded from the following website:

DCF benchmark technical report (PDF)

All the benchmark functions have been implemented in MATLAB code based on PlatEMO [2]. The existing implementations are available in the DCMO benchmark repository.

The original report provides the benchmark definitions. Evaluation budgets and submission requirements for this proposed competition are specified below.

Number of independent runs20

Stopping criterion

Stopping criterion: a maximum number of 100(60τt + 60) fitness evaluations, where 6000 fitness evaluations are given before the first environmental change occurs. After the first environmental change, a maximum of 100τt fitness evaluations are allocated to each subsequent environment, corresponding to 1000 and 3000 fitness evaluations for τt = 10 and τt = 30, respectively.

Environmental change detection and evaluation budgets

In each environment, at most 10% of the population (i.e., 10 individuals) can be used solely for environmental change detection, and the corresponding fitness evaluations are not counted toward the given maximum number of fitness evaluations.

Except for the above change-detection evaluations, all fitness evaluations, including population initialization, offspring evaluation, environmental response, and reevaluation of existing solutions, must be counted toward the corresponding environmental budget.

All participants must strictly follow the above rules on fitness evaluations to ensure the fairness of the competition. Any participant found to have cheated will be disqualified from the competition.

Performance assessment

Metrics and Ranking

MIGD

The MIGD is used to evaluate the performance of an optimizer on each DCMOP. A smaller MIGD value indicates a better performance of the corresponding optimizer.

MHV

The MHV is used to measure the comprehensive performance of an optimizer on DCMOPs. A larger MHV value indicates a better performance of the corresponding optimizer.

Ranking Calculation Rules

Based on the ranking of competition algorithm in the above two indicators, a final Score is calculated to evaluate the performance of the algorithm. The sum of ranks (SR) is calculated based on the rankings obtained using the two performance metrics:

SR= ∑i=140 rankMIGD + ∑i=140 rankMHV

where rank is obtained by the mean and standard deviation value, which is calculated based on the given test function and two performance indicators. The 40 test instances correspond to 10 problems with four combinations of change frequency and severity. When SR values of different algorithms are obtained, scores can be calculated:

Score=(1− SR− SRmin SRmax − SRmin × N−1N )×100

where N denotes the total number of valid participating algorithms, and SRmin and SRmax denote the minimum and maximum SR values among these algorithms, respectively. When SRmax > SRmin, the algorithm with the smallest SR receives a score of 100, while the algorithm with the largest SR receives a score of 100/N.
